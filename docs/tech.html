How MoveGrow
measures movement

MoveGrow turns a home video of a baby into a 3D track of the body, on the iPhone itself, and describes that track with a small set of documented measures. This page explains each step, what the numbers mean and where they stop being reliable.

Source codeRule bookModel notesHow to cite

By Ziqing Shi. This page describes MoveGrow with the InstantHMR pose engine.
Updated November 12, 2026

Overview

A parent films a baby for a minute or two. In each analysed frame, MoveGrow finds the baby, estimates the 3D position of 70 body keypoints with InstantHMR [1] and the MHR body model [2], and re-expresses them in the baby’s own body frame. From that track it computes how much and how fast each limb moves, how far the joints bend, where the head points and how the limbs move together.

A separate rule book compares the track with movement patterns described in published clinical and developmental texts, and says so when it can’t judge. Every rule names its sources and marks each threshold as taken from the literature or as an engineering starting value.

Everything runs on the iPhone. MoveGrow describes movement. It doesn’t score development, screen for conditions or diagnose.

From a frame to a measurement

Five steps, repeated for every analysed frame.

  1. Find the baby

    YOLO26l-pose [4] looks at the frame and returns the largest person. Its box tells the next step where to look. Its own 2D keypoints are kept for comparison and aren’t used in the analysis.

  2. Estimate the body in 3D

    InstantHMR reads two crops of that box, a wide one and a closer one, and predicts the pose and shape of the MHR body model, plus 2D keypoints with an uncertainty for each. The network runs on the iPhone’s Neural Engine.

  3. Fit the pose to the picture

    One Levenberg–Marquardt step moves the predicted pose so that the projected body lands on the network’s own 2D keypoints, each weighted by its confidence. The fit has no learned parameters.

  4. Place 70 keypoints

    The MHR body model turns the fitted parameters into 70 keypoints in the camera’s frame. MoveGrow analyses the body joints. Finger points are drawn and exported, never analysed.

  5. Measure in the baby’s frame

    The keypoints are rotated into the body frame and divided by the torso length. The measures below and the rule book both work on this track.

  • A first pass at 15 frames per second gives results within seconds. A second pass fills in up to the chosen rate (15, 30 or 60 frames per second; 30 by default) and resumes after an interruption.
  • The app doesn’t read the phone’s focal length from the video. It assumes 1.05 times the frame’s diagonal, as InstantHMR does when the focal length is unknown.

Accuracy, and what isn’t known yet

InstantHMR’s authors report its accuracy on two public benchmarks, both recorded with adults moving in everyday scenes [1]. The figures are PA-MPJPE: the mean distance between predicted and true joints after the two poses are aligned, in millimetres. Lower is better.

InstantHMR mediumEMDB-13DPW
Network alone48.8 mm40.0 mm
With the two-crop fit44.4 mm39.3 mm

MoveGrow hasn’t yet measured how well it follows babies. Read the numbers in the app with these limits in mind.

  • Built for people in generalPose models are trained almost entirely on adults and then applied to babies unchanged [5] [6]. Babies’ proportions differ. Work on recovering babies’ and children’s bodies in 3D reports that methods built for adults generalise poorly to them [8], and uses body models made for babies instead [7].
  • One cameraDepth is the model’s estimate, not a measurement, and it is the least certain direction. With an assumed focal length the absolute distance can be off, which is why the measures are in torso lengths.
  • What the camera seesLoose clothes, swaddles and blankets, a caregiver’s hands in the frame and a camera that moves all change the track.
  • Frame rateAt 30 frames per second, rhythms faster than about 15 per second can’t be seen. The rule book asks for at least 24 frames per second before it judges any rhythm.
  • Starting valuesThresholds marked as engineering starting values have been checked only on simulated recordings. They haven’t been calibrated on recordings of real babies.
  • Short videosA minute or two is a small sample of a baby’s day, so one recording is noisy [11]. Patterns that return across several recordings mean more than any single one.

What is checked today: the rule engine in the app gives the same results as its Python reference on 16 simulated recordings with known answers, including ones that must not trigger a rule, such as a shaking camera.

Where the observations come from

Observations are written as rules. They aren’t learned from data. Each rule starts from a description in a published source and turns it into a check on the track: a rhythm in a frequency band, a posture held for a time, a difference between the two sides.

A rule runs only when it can: the baby’s age (corrected for early birth) and position, the frame rate and the share of the body that was tracked all have to fit. Otherwise the rule abstains, and the app lists the check as one it couldn’t run.

FamilyWhat a rule describesExamples of sources
RhythmFast, fine shaking; coarse shaking; bouts of rhythmic kicking or waving; sustained rhythmic jerking on one side[9] [10]
PostureA preference for turning the head to one side; a fencing posture at an age when it has usually faded; legs resting wide apart with little leg movementClinical guidance and reference texts, in the sources file
Side differencesOne arm moving much less than the other, from about 15 weeks of ageReview articles, in the sources file
HandsHands not seen together at the middle of the body after six monthsA public health factsheet, in the sources file

Three more rules exist only in the research reference and don’t run in the app: clusters of brief movements of the whole body, a lasting head tilt, and extended, crossed legs. They stay off until they can be checked against real recordings. Finger and fist postures are never judged.

Clinical assessments of early movement, such as the general movements assessment [12], are made by trained clinicians who judge the quality of whole-body movement. MoveGrow doesn’t perform them or stand in for them.
